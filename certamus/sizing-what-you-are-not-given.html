Sizing what you are not given

Most briefs withhold the number the recommendation depends on. Build it in the open, show the chain, and test whether your ranking survives being wrong.

Assume out loud

Briefs routinely leave out the financials the answer depends on. No revenue, no margin, no cost base, and a question that cannot be answered without them.

There are two responses. Avoid the number, which produces a recommendation nobody can act on. Or build it, say exactly what you assumed, and show the chain. The second is the only one that scores.

State it plainly on the slide: revenue assumed at X, margin at Y, and every figure after this is derived from those two. A panel is not testing whether you had the real number. They are testing whether you knew you did not.

Show the chain, not the conclusion

A sized number is only credible if the reader can walk it.

Forty per cent of revenue is on fixed-price contracts, so six thousand crore. Cost base is ninety-three per cent of that. Fuel and wages are forty-five per cent of the cost base. Costs rise six per cent a year against three per cent priced in, so the gap is seventy-five crore, and at an eighty per cent likelihood that is sixty crore a year.

Every step is arguable and every step is visible. That is the point. A single figure with no chain invites the question "where did that come from", and you lose the next two minutes answering it.

The finding is the ranking

Say this out loud, because it protects everything else: what you are claiming is the order, not the decimals.

If your sizing says the contracts problem is larger than the cyber problem, that ordering is the finding. Whether it is sixty crore or ninety is secondary, and pretending otherwise invites an argument you cannot win.

Then break it yourself

Take the assumption the ranking leans on hardest and move it somewhere plausibly worse. Does the order hold?

If it holds until an input moves by a third, say that on the slide. You have just converted your weakest point into a strength, and pre-empted the question the sharpest judge in the room was about to ask.

If it does not hold, you have found that out in a working session rather than in a final, which is exactly where you want to find it.

Build it in the open, in steps a judge can follow

An estimate a panel cannot follow is worth nothing, however good it is.

The chain that works is short, and each link is a stated assumption rather than a derived one. In Sankriya the largest line went: the fixed-price book is 6,000 crore, the cost base under it is 5,580, fuel and wages are 45% of that at 2,510, costs rise about 6% a year against 3% priced in, so the gap is 75 crore, and at an 0.8 annual likelihood that is 60 crore a year. Four numbers and a multiplication, all visible.

A judge who disagrees can now tell you which link they disagree with, which is a far better conversation than one about whether your answer is plausible.

Break your own ranking first

The useful question is not whether the number is right. It is how wrong it could be before the recommendation changes.

Run it yourself, before the room. Cut the biggest input by a third and see whether the order survives. Put that on the slide. In Sankriya the contract figures had to fall by about a third before the top of the list moved, and saying so removed the obvious attack before anyone could make it.

Say what kind of number it is

Three things get called estimates and they are not the same: a figure from a source, a figure derived from case facts, and a figure the team chose.

Mark them differently. Every rupee figure in the Sankriya register was labelled a team estimate on the slide, and the sources that were real carried their citation. A panel that cannot tell which is which has to assume the worst about all of them.

The ranking is the finding

This is the part that takes the pressure off. You are almost never being asked for the true number, because nobody in the room has it either.

You are being asked which thing is biggest and what follows from that. Say so out loud: the finding is the ranking, not the rupees. It is honest, it is correct, and it moves the argument onto ground you can actually defend.
