About

I came to case competitions from building things that either compute the right number or do not, and that is still how I approach a brief. The first question is never what the answer is. It is which part of the answer would fall over first if someone pushed on it.

So most of my work on a case is upstream of the recommendation: framing the problem so the analysis has somewhere to go, building the model that sizes it, and then trying to break that model before a panel does. In practice that means writing down the test before doing the grouping, printing the scoring scale so it can be argued with, and saying the unflattering thing about our own numbers in the talk rather than the appendix.

The habit comes from the software. A backtest that is not significant is not significant no matter how good the slide looks, and the only useful response is to say so and explain why the thing is still worth funding. Panels are quicker than people expect at spotting the number you skipped past.
