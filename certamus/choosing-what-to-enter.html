Choosing what to enter

You cannot enter everything. Pick for the brief you want to get better at, not for the prize, and treat the first round as the real filter.

Enter fewer, and finish them

The instinct is to register for everything and see what sticks. It produces a lot of abandoned submissions and no improvement, because nothing gets the time it needed.

Pick fewer. Finish them properly, including rehearsal. Two competitions worked end to end teach more than eight half-entered, and a submitted deck you are not proud of costs you the one thing you are building, which is the habit of finishing.

Pick for the brief, not the prize

Prize money is the worst selection criterion available. It is uncorrelated with how useful the competition is to you and strongly correlated with how many teams enter.

Better questions: is this a kind of problem I want to be better at? Will the brief force me to build something I have not built before? Is the judging panel people whose questions I want to hear?

A small competition with a sector you want to learn is worth more than a large one in a sector you already know.

Read the submission requirements before you commit

Round one is usually a deck or an abstract with a hard cap. Ten slides, or three hundred words, or a single page.

Check the cap first, because it determines how much analysis is even usable. A competition that wants three slides is not asking for the work you would do for one that wants fifteen, and teams routinely build the wrong size of answer and then cut badly under deadline.

Be honest about the calendar

Competitions cluster. Two finals in one week is not twice the work, it is considerably more, because rehearsal is what gets squeezed and rehearsal is what decides finals.

Look at the dates before you register, not after. If two overlap, pick one and enter the other next year rather than entering both at reduced strength.

One competition properly beats three badly

This is the only rule on this page that matters.

The teams that place consistently are not the ones entering the most. They are the ones whose fourth competition benefits from everything learned in the first three, which only happens if the first three were actually finished.

Read the brief before you register

A surprising number of teams commit to a competition on the strength of the prize and the poster, then discover the brief is a domain nobody on the team can reason about.

The brief is usually available, or the previous year's is. Read it first. A brief you find interesting on the first reading is one you will still be thinking about on day five, and that matters more than the prize does.

Fit beats prestige, early on

A big competition you place nowhere in teaches less than a small one where you reach a final and get questioned hard for twenty minutes.

Q&A is the part you cannot practise alone, and reaching a late round is the only way to get it. Early on, optimise for rounds survived rather than for the name of the host.

Count the real cost

The entry is a week, not an evening, and the weeks overlap. Three competitions in a month is three weeks where nothing else moves, and the third deck is usually visibly worse than the first.

We have reached a final and not contested it because exams ran the same week, which is written up on People Matrix. That is a better outcome than arriving unprepared, and a worse one than having looked at the calendar first.

Enter the ones you would do anyway

The reliable filter: if the prize were nothing and no recruiter would ever see it, would you still want to spend a week on this problem?

If yes, enter. The work is better, the team argues more usefully, and you learn the thing that lasts, which is how to reason about a domain rather than how to assemble a deck.
