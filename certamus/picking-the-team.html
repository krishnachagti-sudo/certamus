Picking the team

Most competitions allow four, so four of us go. When the limit is three it is the three who started this, because rehearsal time compounds and a settled line-up is worth more than an optimised one.

Four when we can

Most case competitions cap a team at four, and when they do, all four of us enter. There is no selection to make and no case for making one.

That matters more than it sounds. A team that reshuffles per brief spends part of every competition working out how it works. A team that does not gets to spend that time on the case.

Three when we must

When the limit is three it is Krishna, Krishay and Drishti. Not because the brief called for that combination, and not because of any read on who suits which sector. It is the three who started this.

That is worth saying plainly rather than dressing up as a selection philosophy. Teams that claim they assemble per case are usually describing availability after the fact. Ours is a founding trio and a fourth who is part of the team, and when a rule forces a cut the cut follows history rather than a matrix.

Why a settled line-up wins

The parts of a competition that are actually hard are the parts that improve with repetition between specific people: who takes which section, how a handoff sounds, who steps in when a question lands badly.

None of that transfers. It is built between individuals, and it is thrown away every time the line-up changes. Over a season a settled team is rehearsing the things that decide finals while a rotating one is still rehearsing itself.

The cost is real and worth naming. A fixed trio means the fourth person sits out competitions they would have been good at, and the team does not get the benefit of matching a specialist to a brief. We think the compounding is worth more, but it is a trade rather than a free win.

Roles are for the room, not for the work

Everyone works on everything during the week. The roles matter on the day, because a panel asking a question needs one obvious person to answer it and the team needs no visible negotiation about who that is.

Assign them to sections rather than to skills. Whoever built the sizing takes sizing questions, whoever owns the recommendation closes. That is legible from the outside and it is also true, which is why it holds up under pressure.

What a fourth person is actually for

Three people can write a deck. The fourth changes what the team can do in Q&A, because there is someone whose preparation was specifically the defence rather than the build.

When the limit is three, the thing you lose is not a quarter of the analysis. It is the person who was going to take the hardest question, so somebody else has to prepare for it on top of their own section.

Rehearse the handoffs, not just the parts

Most visible failures on stage are at the seams: two people talking at once, a pause while someone finds their place, the same sentence said twice.

Each handoff should be one prepared line, and the person taking over should start with a statement rather than a re-introduction. It sounds small and it is most of what separates a team that looks rehearsed from one that does not.

Disagree before the room, not in it

A team that has genuinely argued about the recommendation gives better answers, because every objection a panel raises has already been raised internally by someone who wanted to win the argument.

Do that on the second day, not the last night. An argument settled the night before the final tends to get settled by whoever is least tired rather than by whoever is right.
