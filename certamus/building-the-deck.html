Building the deck

One idea per slide, the headline doing the work, and an appendix that exists to be opened in Q&A rather than skipped in the talk.

The headline is the slide

Write the top line of every slide as a complete sentence that states the finding. Not "Revenue analysis" but "Forty per cent of revenue sits on contracts that cannot reprice."

Do that for every slide, then read only the headlines in order. If they tell the whole argument on their own, the deck is sound. If they read as a list of topics, you have built a table of contents with charts attached, and a judge skimming ahead will get nothing from it.

This is the single highest-return habit in deck building and almost nobody does it, because topic headers are faster to write and feel more professional.

One idea per slide, and say which

A slide carrying three ideas gets none of them across. Split it.

The test is whether you can say what the slide is for in one clause. If the answer needs an "and", it is two slides. This costs you slide count, which feels expensive under a limit, and buys you comprehension, which is what is being marked.

The appendix is not a dumping ground

An appendix exists for one purpose: to be opened when a judge asks. That makes it a design problem, not a folder.

Put in it the things you expect to be challenged on. Every alternative you tested and rejected, with the numbers. The full assumption list. The sensitivity runs. The workings behind any figure that appears in the main deck without its arithmetic.

Then label the slides so you can find one in four seconds while somebody is waiting. A brilliant appendix you cannot navigate live is worth nothing.

The signal this sends matters as much as the content. A team that shows ten designs they tried, only one of which won, is telling the panel they did the work rather than the one run that flattered them.

Charts carry their own conclusion

A chart with an axis label and no statement makes the reader do the work. Put the finding beside it: not "five-year returns by index" but "nine points of the gap is company size, not factor choice."

And print the value on the bar. Nobody should have to measure a bar against a gridline to know what it says.

What to cut first

When you are over length, the order is: context, methodology, and anything explaining what a framework is. Judges know what a framework is.

What never gets cut is the assumption slide and the sensitivity. Those are the two that make everything else believable, and they are always the first ones a team under pressure reaches for.

The headline carries the slide

If the reader takes only the top line of every slide and puts them end to end, that should be the argument. Titles like "Analysis" or "Market overview" fail this immediately: they name a topic and assert nothing.

Write the finding into the title. "Seventeen risks across four domains; operations carries the most" tells a judge what to look for in the chart below it. "Risk classification" makes them work it out.

Put the weak spots on the results slide

The instinct is to put the caveats after the results, where they read as a retreat from a claim just made.

In Seeking Alpha the results slide carried its own weak spots: the switch adds no return, the alpha after factors is about zero, five years is not long enough for significance. On the same slide as the result. A panel reading that has nothing left to catch you with.

One idea, and the chart that proves it

A slide with three charts has no argument, it has an inventory. Pick the one chart that makes the point and move the others to the appendix, where they exist to be turned to.

If a chart needs more than a sentence of explanation before it means anything, it is the wrong chart for a talk, whatever it is worth in a report.

Design is a clarity budget, not a decoration budget

Every visual decision either makes the argument faster to read or slower. Gradients, shadows, stock photography and animated transitions all cost attention and return nothing.

The things worth spending on are the boring ones: consistent alignment so the eye knows where to go, one accent colour used only for what matters, and enough white space that the important number is obviously the important number.
