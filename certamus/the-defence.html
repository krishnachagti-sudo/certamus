The defence

Most of Q&A should already be answered. We build the likely question list first, fold the answers into the talk, and keep the rest of the defence for what genuinely could not be anticipated.

Answer the questions inside the talk

Q&A is the hardest part of a competition, so most of the work on it happens before it starts.

We write the list of questions a panel is most likely to ask, then build the answers into the presentation itself rather than saving them. The cost is a slightly longer talk. The return is that by the time questions open, the obvious ones are spent, and what is left is narrower and better.

This is why our scripts note that the run time includes the likely questions answered inside it. It is not padding, it is Q&A moved upstream where we control the framing.

The ones you cannot anticipate

Something always survives the list. For those the rule is to say you do not know, then give the bound you can defend and how you would close it.

An admitted range beats a confident guess in both directions. If you are right, the range was honest and the panel saw you reason under uncertainty. If you are wrong, you were wrong about a bound you had already flagged rather than about a number you asserted.

The failure mode is the opposite reflex: producing a specific figure because a specific figure sounds like competence. Panels are quick at spotting a number invented in the moment, and once they spot one they revisit everything else you said.

The part that does not get easier

Q&A is the only genuinely live section. The deck can be rehearsed, the script can be cut to length, the handoffs can be practised. This cannot.

Two things make it harder than it looks. Time pressure, because answers are wanted short and the honest answer is often long. And framing, because a question is sometimes delivered in a way designed to unsettle rather than to inform, and the delivery has to be separated from the question before you answer it.

The only preparation that reliably helps is knowing your own weak numbers better than the panel does. Which is the same discipline as say the weak part first, arriving from the other end.

Pre-empting is not the same as hiding

Answering the likely questions inside the presentation is the core of how this team prepares, and it has a failure mode: a presentation stuffed with pre-empted objections becomes a defence brief nobody asked for.

The test is whether the objection is load-bearing. If the answer changes what the panel should do, it belongs in the talk. If it only proves you thought about it, it belongs in the appendix, and the appendix exists to be opened in Q&A rather than skipped in the talk.

The appendix is a weapon, not a dump

An appendix that is everything you could not fit is useless, because you cannot find anything in it under pressure.

Build it as answers to named questions, one slide each, titled with the question. Then "what happens if the currency assumption is wrong" is a slide you turn to in four seconds, and turning to it is itself an answer: it says the question was expected.

Silence is a tactic they will use

A panel that says nothing after your answer is often testing whether you will keep talking. Most teams do, and the extra sentence is usually the weakest one, because it was not prepared.

Finish the answer and stop. If the silence holds, "happy to go deeper on any part of that" hands the floor back without adding to the argument.

Rehearsing the defence

You cannot rehearse the questions, which is what makes Q&A hard. You can rehearse the shape of an answer until it is automatic under pressure, and you can rehearse the handoffs.

The cheapest useful drill: each person writes the three questions they would least like to be asked, and hands them to someone else on the team to ask cold. The list is almost always right, because the person who built a section knows exactly where it is thin.
