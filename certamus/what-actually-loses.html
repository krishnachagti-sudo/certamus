What actually loses

Not weak analysis. Teams lose by answering a question nobody asked, by hiding the assumption that matters, and by running out of time to rehearse.

Answering the wrong question

The most common loss is a good deck about something adjacent.

It happens because the interesting problem and the asked problem are rarely identical, and the interesting one is more fun to solve. A team finds a rich thread, follows it, and presents an excellent answer to a question the brief did not pose.

The fix is mechanical. At the end, put the prompt next to your headline slides and check them off line by line. If a requirement in the brief has no slide against it, you have found your gap.

The reverse check is worth as much and takes a minute. Go through your slides and ask which line of the brief each one answers. A slide that cannot name its line is a slide you built because it was interesting.

Hiding the load-bearing assumption

Every recommendation rests on one input more than the others. The panel will find it, because they have read the brief more times than you have.

Teams bury it, usually not deliberately. It ends up in the appendix, or stated once quickly, or phrased so it sounds settled. Then a judge surfaces it in Q&A and the answer sounds defensive, because it is.

Put it in the talk instead, name it as the shakiest thing in the model, and show what happens when it moves. You lose nothing you were going to keep.

Running out of rehearsal

The deck finishes late, so the talk is under-practised, so the handoffs are ragged and the timing is wrong and someone overruns and the close gets cut.

The analysis was fine. The delivery of it was not, and only the delivery is marked.

This one is almost universal and almost never admitted. Treat your practice run as the real deadline. Whatever is not finished by then ships unfinished.

Nine recommendations

A deck that recommends everything has decided nothing, and deciding is the skill on trial.

If the analysis produced nine things worth doing, the work is not finished. Say which three, in what order, and what you would drop if the budget halved.

Being unable to say what you do not know

Panels do not expect certainty. They expect you to know the shape of your own uncertainty.

"I do not know that exactly, here is the range I can defend and how I would close it" reads as competence. A confident number invented in the moment reads as the opposite, and once a panel catches one they re-examine everything else you said.

Presenting the analysis instead of the answer

There is a deck shape that feels rigorous and loses anyway. It walks the panel through the method: here is what we gathered, here is how we sorted it, here is what we found, and therefore, on slide nine, here is what you should do.

It loses because a panel decides early whether you have an answer, and this shape withholds one for eight slides. By the time it arrives they have already categorised you as a team that did the work without reaching a conclusion.

Invert it. The answer first, then what it rests on. We open on the answer in every case here, and in Sankriya the first line was that none of the seventeen incidents were the company's fault, which is a claim, not a finding, and it forced everything after it to earn its place.

Winning the argument and losing the clock

Time is the constraint nobody models. A panel that stops you at nine minutes has heard eight minutes of setup and no recommendation, and they will mark what they heard.

Rehearse against a clock that is two minutes shorter than the one you are given, because the real run is always slower than the practice run: you will speak more carefully, someone will fumble a handoff, and a judge may interrupt. If it only fits at your fastest, it does not fit.

Being right about the wrong quantity

A subtler loss: the analysis is correct, the number is accurate, and it is not the number that decides anything.

In Seeking Alpha the tempting analysis was factor selection, because factors are interesting and there is a large literature to show you have read. The finding was that company size explained the period and factor choice mostly did not, so the whole factor discussion was true, careful and irrelevant to the decision.

Before you build a number, ask what you would do differently if it came out twice as large. If the answer is nothing, it is not the number you need.

Letting the panel discover the disagreement

On a team of four, two people often disagree about the recommendation and resolve it by softening the slide until both can live with it.

The panel then reads a hedge, asks which it is, and gets two different answers from two members. That is worse than either position would have been.

Settle it before the room. If you genuinely cannot, put the disagreement on the slide as a decision with a stated trigger, which is an answer. A sentence that means both things is not.
