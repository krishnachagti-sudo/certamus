Say the weak part first

Every analysis has a soft spot. Naming it before the judges find it is not modesty, it is the cheapest way to keep the rest of your numbers believable.

Judges are going to find it anyway

Any model has an assumption holding more weight than the others, a result that is not statistically significant, or a dataset that does not cover what you implied it covers. A panel that finds one you did not mention starts wondering what else is in there.

So the soft spot goes in the talk, in your own words, before anyone asks. Not buried in an appendix, not phrased so carefully it slides past. Stated.

What that sounds like in practice

In a fund pitch, it meant saying out loud that the excess return was not statistically significant over five years, that the trend rule was the best of three settings tested, that the backtest ran on index funds rather than the 28 stocks actually held, and that the rating agency covered only part of the index so a chunk of the book was simply unrated.

In a risk case, it meant printing the scoring scale on the slide so the panel could argue with the numbers, then showing what happened to the ranking when the biggest assumption was cut by a third.

Break your own number before they do

The strongest version of this is not an admission, it is a test. Take the assumption your recommendation leans on hardest, move it to a plausibly worse value, and show what survives.

If the ranking holds, you have converted your weakest point into evidence. If it does not hold, you have found that out before the room did rather than in front of it.

Write down when you would be wrong

Both of these arrive at the same place: a stated condition under which the recommendation should be abandoned. A tracking error above a threshold. A recovery drill that fails its target, gating the next phase of spend.

A recommendation with no failure condition is not a recommendation, it is an opinion. Naming the condition is what makes it checkable, and checkable is the whole argument.

What counts as the weak part

Not every caveat is worth the airtime, and a talk that leads with five of them sounds like a team apologising for its own work.

There is usually one. It is the input that, if it moved, would change the recommendation rather than the decimals. In Sankriya it was the 80% pass-through assumption, because at 40% the payback moved by five months. In Seeking Alpha it was that five years is not long enough for the alpha to be significant, which is the sentence that could have ended the whole argument if a judge had said it first.

Find that one and say it. Everything else is a footnote.

Saying it without undercutting yourself

There is a way to name a weakness that costs you the room, and it is the apologetic version: "we were not able to", "unfortunately we could not", "obviously this is limited".

The version that works states the bound and then what survives it. "Five years is not long enough to call this significant, and the ranking holds in 69 to 79% of the simulated paths, which is the claim we are making." Same fact. One of them sounds like a team that understands its own evidence.

The panel already knows

This is the part teams do not believe until it happens. The judges have read the brief more times than you have, and in a case competition they have often seen six other teams present on it that morning.

They know where the model is soft. The only question is whether you do, and whether you will say so unprompted. That is the whole mechanism: you are not revealing a weakness, you are demonstrating that you found it.
