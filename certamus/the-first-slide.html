The first slide

Restate the question in one line, and let the answer ride on the restatement. If the reframe is right, the recommendation is already implied.

Earn the answer by restating the question

The first slide is not context and it is not an agenda. It is our version of what the brief is actually asking, compressed to a line.

"Seventeen risks, three faults." That is a case brief about a company that had a terrible two years, restated as a claim about where the damage actually came from. Nobody has heard the analysis yet, and the argument is already made.

The test is whether the reframe carries the recommendation inside it. If you can read the first line and correctly guess where the deck lands, it is doing its job. If it only describes the situation, it is a title slide with ambitions.

It is the same move as leading with the answer

A pitch brief looks different on the surface. For a fund the opening line was that matching the benchmark's size mix turns a factor fund that lags into one that beats it by about a point a year after fees.

That reads as the finding rather than a reframe, and it is both. The question handed to us was "pitch a fund". The line restates it as "here is why funds like this usually lose, and it is not what you think" and answers it in the same breath.

So there is one rule rather than two. Restate the question so precisely that the answer follows, then say the answer.

Why not build up to it

Because a panel is not an audience. They are deciding, and they start deciding immediately. A deck that withholds its conclusion for six slides spends those slides being evaluated by people who do not yet know what they are evaluating.

Holding the answer back also costs you the only thing worth having in the room, which is their attention on your reasoning rather than on guessing your destination.

The risk is real: lead with the answer and a weak one gets found fast. We think that is the correct trade. A recommendation that cannot survive being said first was not going to survive Q&A either.

What the first slide is not

It is not the agenda. It is not the team introduction. It is not a restatement of the brief the panel wrote.

Each of those spends the most valuable thirty seconds you have on information the room already has. The agenda in particular is a habit carried in from school presentations, and it tells a judge nothing except that the next eight minutes will be structured.

The reframe is the whole slide

The strongest opener is not the answer stated flatly. It is the answer stated as a correction to what the room expected.

People Matrix opened on the line that the plan about to be recommended handed roughly half the founders' own store profit away, on evidence from two hand-picked outlets. That is an answer and a reframe at once: the panel came expecting a yes-with-conditions and now has to follow the argument to find out why it is not.

One number, chosen carefully

The number under the claim should be the one that makes the claim inevitable, not the largest one you found.

In Sankriya the largest number was the 291 crore of annual expected loss, and the one that did the work was 53%, the share of it coming from risk nobody had priced. The big number describes the problem. The small one picks the fight.

Write it last

The first slide is easiest to write once you know what the deck concluded, and teams that write it first spend the week defending an opener they chose before the analysis.
