Surviving your first Q&A

Most of Q&A is decided before it starts. Build the question list, answer the obvious ones inside the talk, and agree who speaks before anyone is under pressure.

Write the question list first

Before the deck is finished, write down every question you would ask if you were on the panel. Be honest rather than flattering: the list should include the ones you are hoping nobody raises.

Then answer as many as you can inside the presentation itself. It costs a minute of run time and it removes the easiest questions from the room, so what is left is narrower and you have already framed it.

This is the highest-leverage preparation available, and it is invisible. A panel does not notice that their first three questions were pre-empted. They notice that the session felt sharp.

Decide who answers, before you are asked

Four people and one question produces either two people starting at once or nobody starting at all. Both look bad, and both are avoidable.

Agree the routing in advance. Numbers questions go to whoever built the model. Recommendation questions go to whoever presented it. Anything hostile or open-ended goes to one nominated person, so the decision is already made when the room is tense.

One person answers. The others stay quiet unless invited. A team piling on to one question reads as a team that does not trust its own answer.

Answer the question, then stop

The most common Q&A failure is talking past the answer.

Say the thing, give the reason, stop. Silence after a complete answer feels much longer to you than to the panel. Filling it is how teams talk themselves into a qualification they did not need to make.

What to do when you do not know

Say so, then give the bound.

"We did not size that. My estimate is it sits between X and Y, and the way to settle it is Z." That is a complete, professional answer, and it is far stronger than a specific figure produced under pressure.

Never invent a number in Q&A. It is the single fastest way to lose a panel, because the moment they doubt one figure they revisit all of them.

Separate the question from how it was asked

Some questions arrive sharply. Occasionally that is a test of composure rather than a real objection.

Take the content, leave the tone. Answer as though it had been asked neutrally. If there is a fair point inside an aggressive question, agree with that part out loud before you respond to the rest. Agreeing with a true criticism costs you nothing and buys you the room.

The question behind the question

Most questions are not really about what they ask. "How did you get the growth rate?" is usually "do you know this number is load-bearing?" and the answer that satisfies it names the sensitivity, not the arithmetic.

Answer the literal question first, in one sentence, then the one underneath it. Reversing that order sounds like you are avoiding the literal one.

When you are wrong

It happens, and it is survivable if you do it in one move. Say that they are right, say what it changes, and say what survives.

"That is a fair catch. It moves the payback from month twenty-five to about month thirty. The ranking does not change, because the gap between the top two is larger than that error." A panel will take that. What they will not take is three minutes of defending a number you already know is wrong.

The teams that handle this well are the ones who worked out in advance which of their numbers could be wrong and by how much, which is the same work as sizing what you are not given.

Questions you cannot answer

Some questions are outside what you built, and the honest answer is short.

Name what you would need, say how long it would take, and say what you would expect to find. "We did not model competitor response. It needs their pricing history, which is a day of work, and I would expect it to shrink the advantage rather than remove it." That is a professional answer. "Good question, we did consider that" is not, and panels have heard it several hundred times.

The one who has not spoken

Panels notice when one person answers everything. It reads as a team with one person who understands the work.

Route deliberately: whoever owns the section owns the question. If a question lands on the wrong person, hand it across in the open. "Krishay built the sizing, so he should take that." That costs three seconds and buys the impression it is meant to buy, because it is true.
